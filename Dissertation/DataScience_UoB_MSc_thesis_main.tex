\chapter{Introduction: Contextual Background}
\label{chap:introduction}
Chronic pain is a common and complex condition that persists for longer than three months, affecting 30-33\% of the population worldwide and 1 in 3 adults in the UK \cite{shetty_chronic_2025}. A meta-analysis of 19 populations by \cite{fayaz_prevalence_2016} showed that 43.5\% of the UK population (28 million adults) experience chronic pain and 14.3\% (8 million adults) live with moderate or severe disabling chronic pain. Due to the ageing population in England, chronic pain is estimated to grow by 1.7 million by 2024 \cite{toby_watt_health_2023}. Patients with chronic pain can also experience impacts on their quality of life, daily living activities, and psychological well-being \cite{andersen_psychosocial_2014}. The treatment for chronic pain is limited because the underlying cause of this condition is unknown. The standard treatments for acute pain are opioids, but they are not considered safe for long term use for chronic pain due to the shifts in the physiology of the nociceptors. 
Chronic pain is caused by injury or disease in the nervous system and is associated with persistent hyperactivity of nociceptors, which are specialised to detect damaging stimuli and/or inflammation in rodents or humans \cite{walters_persistent_2023}. Therefore, a constant activation of these nociceptors, especially the C-fibre nociceptor, is a driver of prolonged pain and can lead to maladaptive neuroplasticity. Therefore, measuring the abnormal activities of these nociceptors could reveal and allow for precise pain targeting \cite{ossipov_central_2010}. These nociceptor activities can be recorded using microneurography, a technique where an electrode is inserted through the skin into a nerve bundle to record action potentials from a single fibre of interest. However, the spike patterns of these C-fibre nociceptors in the microneurography recordings are challenging because of the small spike amplitude, low signal-to-noise ratio, the conduction velocity and activity dependence, and their waveform shapes are similar between different fibres \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}. Comprehensive spike detection and sorting analysis are therefore required. Many studies have researched and developed methods for extracellular spike detection and sorting for multi-electrode data, but not many focus on single electrodes where the spatial information is absent, and the discharge rates of unmyelinated peripheral nerve fibres are lower.

\label{sec:extracellular}
Extracellular recordings monitor the activity of single or multiple neurons by placing electrodes to measure action potentials from sensory fibres, and it is commonly used to investigate nociceptive activity \cite{kechris_removing_2021, guo_extracellular_2020}. When investigating pain, electrophysiological recordings of peripheral sensory activity can measure abnormal excitability and activity of nociceptor fibres which appear to drive pain in people with chronic pain \cite{themistocleous_defining_2026}. However, identifying these events in such recordings is challenging, especially in a single electrode, because the recorded signal contains multiple action potentials from different fibres with similar characteristics and background noise \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026, lecoq_removing_2021, kechris_removing_2021}. A similar issue is reported in human microneurography, where automated spike sorting is challenging because a single recording site can capture activity from multiple C-fibres \cite{troglio_action_2024}.

\label{sec:detection}
Because these recordings record the activity of several sources, spike sorting is essential to detect the action potentials and assign each to its unit before any firing patterns analysis \cite{rey_past_2015}. Therefore, spike sorting is divided into two stages: detection, which is identifying when a spike occurs, and sorting or classification, which is determining which unit produced it.  

Most spike sorting analysis pipelines follow a common sequence \cite{rey_past_2015}: starting with band-pass filtering to isolate the spike frequency band; the spikes are then detected, usually by amplitude thresholding; then a waveform is extracted around each spike; and then features are computed for dimensionality reduction, for example by using principal component analysis (PCA) \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004}; and then the spikes are clustered in this feature space, where each cluster corresponds to a putative unit. According to \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}, PCA reduces dimensionality but might discard discriminative details that raw waveforms retain, which was found to perform better. 
Overall, the feature-based spike sorting pipeline is based on the assumption that spikes from different units form distinctive clusters in the chosen feature space \cite{lewicki_review_1998}. This assumption could be true when the units have distinct waveform shapes, such as spikes from different spatial positions captured by multiple electrodes, yielding a distinct multi-channel signature \cite{harris_accuracy_2000}. Therefore, modern automated sorters like WaveClus \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004} and Kilosort and MountainSort produce sufficient separation. However, this assumption of cluster separation fails when waveforms are similar between units, such as those in single-channel recordings, which is discussed in the following section. 

Spike detection is an important step, as the quality of the algorithm influences the performance of the next steps, such as spike sorting and classification, so any errors in detection of the numbers and location of the target spikes would inevitably propagate through all the later analyses in the analysis \cite{azami_extracellular_2015}. Multiple reasons can make spike detection difficult; the recorded spikes are corrupted by non-target neurons' activities, background noise and recording hardware noise. Moreover, the activities of nearby neurons in the recording may merge as noise that correlates with the target signal \cite{azami_extracellular_2015}.  

Single-channel lacks this spatial information, so algorithms rely mostly on temporal voltage dynamics such as waveform amplitude, shape, latency, and frequency content. 
This involves setting a fixed voltage threshold, classifying any crossings as candidate spikes and extracting a short segment of the recorded voltage around these events for subsequent spike detection, which then can be extracted for waveform analysis, classification or spike sorting \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}. For a single spike not to be counted more than once as a separate event, a refractory period or a shadow period is enforced between the detected spike and the next spike \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}. 
A fixed-amplitude threshold detection is simple and commonly used, but its performance depends on the user's selected threshold, so a threshold that is too high can cause low-amplitude spikes to be undetected, which can cause a single unit to be missed. If a threshold is too low, it can allow the detection of noise and non-sortable activities, which would increase the fraction of time spent in the refractory period and, in turn, reduce detection for larger spikes \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}. 
Thus, the selected threshold influences the detected events and later spike classification and sorting stages. Therefore, amplitude thresholding detects spikes by their size, which is limited when the spikes vary in amplitude, especially in microneurography, where spikes from C-fibres have a low signal-to-noise ratio \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}. An alternative method to amplitude thresholding is template matching, where the spikes are detected by their shape rather than its ampliude. A template of the target waveform correlates with the signal, and detections are the positions of high similarity \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}. Unlike amplitude thresholding, template matching can detect low-amplitude spikes that some amplitude threshold would miss \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}, and by normalising the similarity score (normalised cross-correlation), it becomes independent of amplitude, so small or large spikes with the same shape are detected equally. Nonetheless, its performance depends on how distinct the target's spike shape is from other non-targets. 
For the spike sorting stage, unsupervised clustering is commonly used, which is where the spikes are grouped by similarity would labels \cite{lewicki_review_1998}. This method is suitable if ground truth labels are not available; however, when the spikes from different units overlap in feature space, clustering does not separate them. The alternative method is used when ground truth labels are available: classification, where the model learns a decision boundary from the labelled target and nontarget spikes \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}. 
Before the sorting stage, features have to be extracted to reduce the dimensionality of the waveforms. The most commonly used method for dimensionality reduction is PCA, where the waveforms are projected into the direction of greatest variance \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004}. But this can discard discriminative details such as the fine features that can distinguish similar spikes \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004}. The alternative is to use the raw waveform directly as features similar to \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}, who compared feature sets for spike sorting and found that raw waveform features performed better than PCA. However, \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} compared this for single-channel analysis, so dimensionality reduction could be more useful for multi-channel analysis, where PCA can capture the cross-channel spatial signature.

\chapter{Execution}
\label{chap:execution}
This chapter describes the data and the methods used to detect, classify and denoise the target fibre's spikes in a single channel. It first introduces the recording and how the target unit was identified, then estimates the noise floor. It then describes the detection pipeline in three stages: building and matching a spike template, and constraining detections by their timing after the stimulus. It then describes the two classifiers used on the detection survivors: a one-class and two-class support vector machine. Finally describes three denoising methods: Wavelet, Dense Autoencoder and Convolutional Autoencoder to evaluate each method and to assess whether denoising improves detection or classification, and the metrics used throughout. 
\section{The data: rat saphenous nerve microneurography}
This project uses an extracellular microneurography recording from the peripheral nerve of a rat, sampled at 30 kHz over approximately 2.3 hours. The nerve carries many afferent fibres, so the electrode records the action potentials of many units at once, superimposed on background noise. Recordings were made using two multi-electrode sensor units placed at different positions along the nerve, each containing several
channels corresponding to slightly different electrode locations. Eight channels carry manually labelled ground truth spikes (CH3, CH8, CH9, CH10, CH14, CH19, CH25, CH30). During the recording, an electrical stimulus (Pain stimulus) was delivered repeatedly, giving 2,310 stimulation pulses (TTL) at a normal rate of around 0.25 Hz. The target fibre responds to each stimulus by firing at an approximately fixed latency. The ground truth spike times were marked manually by a human annotator \ref{fig:raw signal}, who only
labelled recognisable nerve responses; therefore, the annotations are conservative, and not every response is labelled. Each spike is marked at the point where the signal crosses zero on its descending slope.

\subsection{Target unit and channels}
The lab identified a single target unit as a strong unit with large identifiable spikes on multiple channels. This project analyses five of these channels: 9, 14, 19, 25, and 30, with a strong signal, and each is analysed independently. 
The spike latencies of the target unit were manually annotated on each channel; consequently, they are treated as reference annotations rather than complete ground truth, as they were manually identified and may contain errors. 
Only a 15-minute window was used for analyses, beginning at the 900th stimulus pulse. 
This window length was chosen because the target unit's latency remained stable; a longer window showed activity-dependent slowing that spreads the latency and prevents a fixed latency window from capturing the spikes. The signals were preprocessed by the recording laboratory, so it has been band-pass filtered 300-6000 Hz, common average referenced, and stimulus blanked. 
\section{Noise estimation and preprocessing}
As mentioned, no further filtering was applied in this project, because the recording given was already bandpass-filtered, with stimulation artefact blanked (set to zero). 
The target units are small, only about two to three times the amplitude of the background noise; consequently, they do not stand out clearly and can not be isolated by amplitude threshold alone. Therefore, removing the noise, detecting and classifying the target unit's spikes are the central challenges this chapter tries to address. The noise level is estimated using the median absolute deviation (MAD) \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004, park_deep_2019}:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\operatorname{median}(|x|)}{0.6745}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the signal. The median is used instead of standard deviation because the spikes are infrequent, so the median does not inflate the estimate the way standard deviation would; dividing by 0.6745 rescales the value to be comparable to a standard deviation under an assumption of Gaussian noise \cite{drebitz_optimizing_2019}. MAD is an estimate, so it assumes the signal is mostly noise and approximately Gaussian \cite{coupe_robust_2010}. But because it is applied identically to every signal, it is a valid basis for comparison.
\section{Leakage safe evaluation protocol}
For unbiased performance, a chronological split was used on the recording by dividing the recording into a development set on the first 70\% [3602.7-4232.7 s] and a held-out evaluation on the last 30\% of the recording [4232.7-4502.7 s]. All hyperparameter tuning was conducted on the development set, and the evaluation set was scored once to prevent data leakage and to avoid overfitting \cite{rashid_spike_2025}. All component that could carry information across the split was fitted on the development set only; the spike template was built from target spikes in the development annotations split only; the autoencoders were trained on synthetic data derived from the development set. Detection parameters like shape similarity and latency window were selected by maximising F1 on the development set. The classifiers and the two-class classifier were fitted on the development survivors only with fixed hyperparameters and scored once on the evaluation survivors. The confidence intervals were computed by stratified bootstrap resampling of the evaluation candidates.  

\section{Detection}
\subsection{Template matching and spike detection}
The spike template was built by averaging the annotated unit's spikes on the development set, to prevent leakage in later stages. For each marked spike, a 90-sample window (45 samples on either side of the spike trough) is then extracted from the signal and then averaged to give the unit's mean waveform. 
Before averaging, each spike is aligned on a common reference point within a short search region around the marked time, so that the same feature falls at the same position in every window. Without this, the waveform would sit at a slightly different offset, and the average would be blurred. Trough alignment is the standard approach in the literature, though it is sensitive to noise, as the exact location of the trough can shift slightly between spikes \cite{lee_non-linear_2021}. 
The template is slid across the signal to locate spikes that match the unit's waveforms, then a similarity score is computed at every position. This score is the Pearson correlation coefficient between the template and the signal window at each position, which is an approach following \cite{park_deep_2019}, who applied it for spike classification on single-electrode extracellular recordings: 
\begin{equation}
    r_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n} (t_j - \bar{t})(x_{i,j} - \bar{x}_i)}
    {\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n} (t_j-\bar{t})^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_{i,j} - \bar{x}_i)^2}}
\end{equation}
Where $t_j$ are the templates, $x_{i,j}$ are the signal samples in the window at position $i$, $\bar{t}$ and $\bar{x}$ are their mean, and $n$ is the template length. The score $r_i$ lies in [-1,1], and 1 means a perfect match in shape; because the score is normalised, it measures waveform shape independently of amplitude, so a small spike and a large spike with the same shape would score equally. A threshold on a normalised shape similarity score to separate the target units from other detections is used by \cite{laboy-juarez_normalized_2019}, although this paper uses cosine similarity across multi-channel templates rather than the single-channel Pearson correlation used here. 
Latency constraints were applied on the detection search space following \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}. The target fibre fires at around the same latency after each stimulus, which is around 100 ms, so a detection's post-stimulus latency indicates whether it belongs to the target unit or not. For each spike, the latency was computed as the interval from the most recent preceding stimulus; the median latency across the development spikes was around 100 ms. So, a detection is only retained if its post-stimulus latency falls within a window centred on this median. 
Following the leakage safe protocol, both the shape similarity and the latency window width were selected only on the development set by sweeping candidate values and choosing the combination that maximises the development $F_1$ score. These then were frozen and applies to the held out evaluation set. 

\section{Denoising methods}
Three denoisers are used to remove the noise from the recording: wavelet, dense and convolutional autoencoder. 
\subsection{Wavelet}
Wavelet denoising is a signal processing method that works by transforming the signal into wavelet coefficients to remove or shrink the small coefficients that represent noise, and then it reconstructs the signal from what remains \cite{wei_wavelet_2005, donoho_minimax_1998}.  For this project, SWT was used over DWT for the reasons mentioned in \ref{chap:background}. The signal was decomposed using Daubechies 4 to decomposition level 4 to give approximation and detail at each level. Then the noise level was estimated at each level using the same MAD estimator on the detail coefficients at each level. A universal threshold was applied using the VisuShrink formula:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda = \sigma \sqrt{2 \ln N}
    \label{VisuShrink}
\end{equation}
Where $N$ is the number of coefficients at that level \cite{donoho_minimax_1998}. The detail coefficents were shrunk using a soft threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \eta(x, \lambda) = \text{sign}(x)\, \max(|x| - \lambda,\, 0)
    \label{Soft thresholding}
\end{equation}
This then shrinks the coefficients towards zero instead of removing them, and the approximation coefficients were unchanged. The denoised signal is then reconstructed back from the cleaned coefficients to a time-domain signal by inverse SWT. To allow for smoother reconstruction and avoid sharp discontinuities, the soft threshold was chosen over a hard threshold \cite{zhang_improved_2019}. 
\subsection{Dense and Convolutional Autoencoders}
Two autoencoders were used to denoise the signal: dense and convolutional autoencoders. Both were trained on the same development set and applied to the evaluation set in the same way; they only differ in their internal architecture. During training, autoencoders generally require clean signals as a target reference so the model can calculate its reconstructive loss \cite{vincent_extracting_2008, kechris_removing_2021}. With real extracellular recordings, there is no clean reference, so training pairs were generated using synthetic pairs from the real spike template. The continuous signal was divided into 60 sample windows; each window was created as one of two types: a window that contains the template spike from the development set or only a noisy window. In the window with a spike, the template was randomly shifted and scaled so the model learns to see spikes at different positions and amplitudes. For each clean window, Gaussian noise was then added to produce the noisy input, giving pairs of noisy input and clean target. Both autoencoders used the Adam optimiser for training to minimise the mean squared error (MSE) between the reconstruction output and the clean target. 
To denoise the recording, the trained models were applied to overlapping windows sliding across the signal. Each of the windows was normalised by the MAD noise level before the model to match the training normalisation, then rescaled back, and he outputs were averaged where windows overlapped. 
The differences between Dense and convolutional are that Dense uses fully connected layers that compress the 60-sample window to 30, then to a 15-unit latent space \cite{li_aecuration_2025}. Whereas convolutional replaces the fully connected layers with 1D convolutional layers, where each layer slides a small filter (kernel size 3) across the window to detect local spike-shaped patterns where they occur in the window \cite{kechris_removing_2021, li_accurate_2020}. This handles spikes at different positions better than dense. 

The quality of each denoiser was assessed by measuring the noise level of each denoised signal using MAD \ref{MAD noise estimate}. However, reduction in noise level alone does not indicate a better signal for spike detection or sorting; focusing only on noise removal ignores whether the spike morphology is preserved \cite{baldazzi_systematic_2020}. However, most studies like \cite{baldazzi_systematic_2020} use RMSE to quantify morphology preservation against a clean reference. Here, the recoding is real and noisy annotated spikes with no clear reference to compare against. So, here peak-to-peak was measured following \cite{adam_comparative_2025, harris_accuracy_2000} as a quantitative waveform feature to assess the spike amplitude after denoising. Peak-to-peak evaluates the denoisers by quantifying the absolute difference between the maximum and minimum values of a waveform before and after filtering \cite{harris_accuracy_2000}. For each of the target spikes, the peak-to-peak amplitude was computed from the aligned waveforms. Amplitude retention was then expressed as the mean peak-to-peak amplitude of each denoised signal relative to the raw signal. 
\section{Classification}
Two support vector machine (SVM) classifiers with radial basis function (RBF) kernels were applied to the development set through aligned waveforms, following the raw waveforms approach of \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}, which found that raw waveforms performed better than PCA-derived features. One-class and two-class SVMs were used for this analysis. The difference between the two SVMs: one-class was trained on only the target survivors of the development set, and two-class was trained on both target and non-target survivors of the development set. Both SVMs were trained with fixed hyperparameters (nu = 0.1, C = 1.0). Then, each fitted classifier with its feature scaler was applied once on the held-out evaluation set survivors. This ensures that no candidate is scored by a model trained on to avoid data leakage. 
\subsection{One-class SVM}
One-class SVM is a novelty detection machine learning method that learns a decision function to classify raw data as similar to or different to the training set \cite{yang_efficient_2021}. The one-class model was trained on the target survivors in the development set waveforms and then evaluated once on the held-out evaluation set target and non-target survivors. Because the model was trained on the development set and scored only on the evaluation set, no waveform appears in both training and testing. So, each evaluation candidate received a single prediction from a fixed model. An RBF was used ($\gamma$ = scale), with nu ($\nu$) fixed at 0.1 a priori to avoid tuning the hyperparameter on the evaluation data. This prevents biased estimates of how well the model would generalise, because nu tests the expected fraction of training points allowed outside the learned boundary and the fraction of support vectors in the interval [0, 1]; and gamma controls the influence of each training example \cite{moctezuma_multi-objective_2020}. Studies such as \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} use the $F_1$ score as a primary measure of the one-class model. However, $F_1$ relies on binary predictions that are evaluated at a fixed decision boundary, so they are sensitive to the boundary tunning hyperparameter. The nu hyperparameter in one-class controls the upper bound on the fraction of outliers and the lower bound on support vectors, influencing how tightly the boundary shrinks or how widely it expands around the data \cite{scholkopf_estimating_2001}. According to \cite{tax_support_2004}, altering this boundary dictates the trade-off between false positives and false negatives, so if nu does not exactly match the true contamination rate, the target precision-recall balance would shift and cause the discrete $F_1$ score to fluctuate. For this analysis, these metrics were used, but not as the primary measurement for SVM performance; instead, ROC-AUC is used with a bootstrap confidence interval. This is because ROC-AUC evaluates the continuous decision output across all thresholds, so adjusting nu does shift the decision boundary, but it preserves the candidate ranking order, making ROC-AUC invariant to nu tuning \cite{tax_support_2004, abdelhamid_balancing_2024}. 
Because one-class RBF classifiers are sensitive to feature scaling \cite{juszczak_feature_2002, feragen_adaptive_2015}, the one-class SVM features were not standardised. Standardising these waveforms divides low-variance tail points by near-zero standard deviation. This amplifies background noise, corrupts the waveform's morphology and inflates Euclidean distance in the RBF kernel, which causes the boundary to reject almost all candidates, including target ones. 
\subsection{Two-class SVM}
Unlike one-class SVM, two-class SVM is a supervised machine learning model that works by separating data points into two distinct groups to find a boundary between them \cite{krebs_applying_2024}. So, here it's trained on both target and non-target survivors' waveforms of the development set to learn the boundary the separate these two classes. Compared to the non-target survivors, the target survivors are a minority, so class imbalance was handled by class weighting rather than resampling. For the two classes, the features were standardised using a StandardScaler fit on the development set and then applied to the evaluation set, in contrast to one-class SVM. Two-class SVM was standardised following the recommendation of \cite{carugo_users_2010}. Standardisation here is safe because the two-class training set is heterogeneous, containing both target and no-target spikes and diverse background noise waveforms, so the feature standard deviation remains non-zero across all points, eliminating the risk of near-zero division. This in turn allows for balanced feature scales to be standardised and improves the distance between the decision boundary and support vectors without amplifying baseline noise or distorting kernel distance metrics \cite{cortes_support-vector_1995}. 
As with one-class, the C parameter was fixed at 1.0 a priori to avoid tuning on the evaluation set. The classifier was trained on the development survivors and applied once to evaluation survivors, so that each evaluation candidate is predicted once from one fixed model and the performance is then measured by ROC-AUC, precision, recall, and F1. 
\section{Evaluation metrics}
 To evaluate detection and classification performance on raw and denoised signals, metrics derived from the confusion matrices were used, along with ROC-AUC with bootstrap confidence intervals for classification. 
 \subsection{Confusion matrix}
 The confusion matrices count true positives (TP), false positives (FP), false negatives (FN), and true negatives (TN) \cite{singh_diagnosing_2021}. From these, precision [TP/(TP+FP)], recall [TP(TP+FN)] and their harmonic mean (the $F_1$ score) were computes. The false-positive-to-true-positive ratio (FP/TP) was also computed for detection following \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} as an indicator of the false-positive burden. 
 \subsection{ROC-AUC}
 To evaluate the classifiers' performance, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used as an indicator of the classifiers' discriminatory power and the model's ability to separate classes across all decision thresholds \cite{hanley_meaning_1982}. ROC-AUC has a fixed chance level of 0.5, so an AUC of 0.5 means no discrimination between classes (equivalent to random guessing), and a higher AUC means the stronger the discriminatory power \cite{hosmer_applied_2013}. As mentioned above, because ROC-AUC is invariant to the class imbalance between the minority target and majority non-target was chosen as the primary metric for the classifiers. It is fixed chance reference makes it straightforward to assess whether a classifier performs above chance. 
 \subsection{Bootstrap confidence intervals}
 Each ROC-AUC is a point estimate from one candidate set. The uncertainty is quantified with 95\% confidence intervals computed by bootstrap resampling \cite{efron_resampling_2001}, as used by \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} for spike sorting performance. The difference is that \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} quantified performance uncertainty around $F_1$ score, while here around ROC-AUC. The evaluation candidates were resamples with replacement 2000 times, and the ROC-AUC was computed for each resample. Then the 95\% intervals were taken as the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the resample values. Any resample that only contained one class were skipped. To interpret the results, a narrow interval indicates AUC is likely accurate, while a wide interval indicates the AUC values are less reliable \cite{corbacioglu_receiver_2023}.

Channel-to-channel variation [TO DO]



Several reasons and mechanisms reported in the literature for denoising increasing the number of false positives with some denoisers and some channels, and these fall into two categories. These false positives could be genuine biological spikes that were buried by the noise and were detected by the template matching after denoising, or artefacts introduced by the denoisers themselves.
According to \cite{bengio_generalized_2013}, denoising autoencoders can learn to pull corrupted points back onto the manifold via a learned vector field, so when the model receives unstructured noise, it can reshape that noise into the nearest pattern, which, in this case, is spikes. This could be the reason for the higher number of false positives compared to raw. Furthermore, denoising can diminish the spikes' fine features that help distinguish them from neighbouring units \cite{baldazzi_systematic_2020}, which in turn causes non-target spikes to resample the target template. If the additional detections (false positives) by the denoisers are actual spikes, they should look like the spike's waveforms in the raw signal; and if they are artefacts, they should be absent or near flat in the raw signal since they were created. This check was only done on ch19 for the dense autoencoder, where the FP/TP ratio increased from 4.73 to 7.00 with 11 additional detections. These additional detections have a mean trough of -11.77 in the raw signal, similar to the target spikes, indicating that these extra false positives might be spike-like events rather than artefacts produced by the denoiser. However, this can not rule out any other possibilities, as only the mean trough spikes were checked and only on one channel with one denoiser. Nonetheless, in a single channel, spikes do overlap, have similar waveform shapes and latency, and without the spatial information that of multi-channel analysis, it is difficult to distinguish target spikes from non-target \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}.

Across all denoisers and channels, the confidence intervals are wide, spanning from 0.2 to 0.8. These intervals were computed using stratified bootstrap resampling, so this width is not due to shifting class balance across resamples. However, two factors account for this wide interval: first, the small evaluation set, which was around 60 survivors per channel, of which only around 14 to 22 are target, limiting the statistical power and widening the intervals. The second factor is that the target and non-target waveforms overlap, making it difficult for the classifier to separate them even on a denoised signal, because non-target events appear to have a spike shape similar to the target \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026, hill_quality_2011}. The confusion matrix \ref{tab:confusion} supports this; the classifier accepts non-target survivors as targets, returning precision [0.13 to 0.40]; false positives exceed true positives, which confirms that classification is not discriminating between target spike and non-target spike and that this is consistent between channels. 
These results suggest that the raw waveform features and the SVM tested here are unable to separate target units from non-target spikes on a single channel. This is a negative finding, suggesting that waveform shape alone can not resolve this contamination. Denoising the signal does not change this conclusion. Although some point estimates differ slightly, none of these differences seems meaningful. For example, the two-class ROC-AUC for the conv denoiser reached 0.61 on channels 14 and 30; their confidence intervals still include 0.5 (0.42-0.82) and (0.42-0.80); does not indicate above-chance performance. Similarly, the channel-to-channel variation observed is expected because of the small evaluation set, resulting in ROC-AUC estimates that are quite uncertain, so a difference such as 0.44 vs 0.68 could just be sampling variability rather than evidence that some channels provide better class separation. Furthermore, because multiple channels, denoising and classification conditions are being evaluated, some ROC-AUC may rise due to sampling variability and should therefore be interpreted with caution \cite{sullivan_facts_2021}. 
\section{Summary of the results}
The overall detection performance showed variation across conditions \ref{tab:Summary}. The FP/TP ratio for raw was 3.69 $\pm$ 0.70, compared with 3.63 $\pm$ 1.35 for wavelet, 4.01 $\pm$ 1.72 for dense and 3.61 $\pm$ 0.65 for conv. Consequently, denoising does not give a consistent reduction in the false positive to true positive ratio. The lowest ratio is observed with conv, while the highest is with dense; however, the differences are small relative to the variability across channels. 
Because both classifiers were performed on the survivors retained from detection, changes at the detection stage give important context for the classification results. 

Similarly, the overall performance of classification across the five channels showed that denoising does not produce consistent improvement in classification performance \ref{tab:Summary}. Dense denoising for one-class classification shows the highest mean ROC-AUC (0.547 $\pm$ 0.059), compared with 0.518 $\pm$ 0.051 for the raw condition. In contrast, for two-class SVM, the conv denoising showed the highest mean ROC-AUC (0.536 $\pm$ 0.082), compared to 0.499 $\pm$ 0.066 for the raw condition, and Wavelet showed lower mean ROC-AUC (0.435 $\pm$ 0.073). However, the effect of denoising on classification differed between channels and denoisers \ref{fig:classification heatmap}. For example, one-class ROC-ayc on channel 30 increased from 0.52 in the raw condition to 0.68 with wavelet-denoised signal and 0.65 with conv. Whereas the ROC-AUC on channel 19 decreased to 0.37 with cnov denoising. Similarly, for two-class SVM, conv increased ROC-AUC on channel 25 from 0.40 to 0.59, and reduced to 0.35 with wavelet.

\section{Limitations}
Several limitations must be considered when interpreting the results. The fixed latency window used here assumes the latency is constant, but over longer recordings, activity-dependent slowing (ADS) spreads the latency. Here the method is restricted to a 15 minutes winodow where the latency is stable; longer and more active periods would require latency tracking \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}. This leads to the next limitation: because of this restriction of 15 minutes window, the held- out evaluation set is small (~60 survivors per channel, of which only ~14-22 are targets). This limits the statistical power and gives wide confidence intervals that are less informative. The at-chance findings are consistent with chance rather than proven at chance, so a larger evaluation set would tighten the intervals and would give better interpretability. 
This study only tests three denoisers (Wavelet SWT, dense autoencoder and convolutional autoencoder). Other denoising methods such as other wavelet families, other architectures, and self-supervised methods may perform differently. So, the conclusion that denoising does not help is limited to these three denoisers and implementations. 
The SVM hyperparameters in this study were fixed a priori, not tuned, so tuning the hyperparameters may change the performance. 
The target spikes are manual annotations, not perfect ground truth, so some detections scored as false positives might include unlabelled target spikes, which would affect the evaluation. 
Because the data used for the analysis is real, denoising quality was assessed by amplitude preservation and noise level reduction relative to raw, so no reconstruction error RMSE against clean signal. Therefore, this is a practical proxy, not the ideal measure. 
Only the raw waveform features were used following \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}, which found that raw feature waveforms performed better than PCA. However, for this data, a different feature representation might change the classification performance. The findings of this analysis are from one recording with a specific signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and fibre count condition; different recordings with higher SNR and fewer fibres might show different results, as \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} found variation across recordings.

\section{Future work}

{\bf A compulsory chapter,  roughly 10\% of the total page-count}
\vspace{1cm} 

\noindent
The concluding chapter(s) of a dissertation are often underutilized because they're 
too often left too close to the deadline: it is important to allocate enough time and 
attention to closing off the story, the narrative, of your thesis.

Again, there is no single correct way of closing a thesis. 

One good way of doing this is to have a single chapter consisting of three parts:

\begin{enumerate}
\item (Re)summarise the main contributions and achievements, in essence
      summing up the content.
\item Clearly state the current project status (e.g., ``X is working, Y 
      is not'') and evaluate what has been achieved with respect to the 
      initial aims and objectives (e.g., ``I completed aim X outlined 
      previously, the evidence for this is within Chapter Y'').  There 
      is no problem including aims which were not completed, but it is 
      important to evaluate and/or justify why this is the case.
\item Outline any open problems or future plans.  Rather than treat this
      only as an exercise in what you {\em could} have done given more 
      time, try to focus on any unexplored options or interesting outcomes
      (e.g., ``my experiment for X gave counter-intuitive results, this 
      could be because Y and would form an interesting area for further 
      study'' or ``users found feature Z of my software difficult to use,
      which is obvious in hindsight but not during at design stage; to 
      resolve this, I could clearly apply the technique of Bloggs {\em et al.}.
\end{enumerate}

Alternatively, you might want to divide this content into two chapters: a penultimate chapter with a title such as ``Further Work" and then a final chapter ``Conclusions". Again, there is no hard and fast rule, we trust you to make the right decision.
