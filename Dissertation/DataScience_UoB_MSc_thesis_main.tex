This chapter should describe the project context, and motivate each of

summarises the aims, objectives, {\bf and achievements}\/ of your work.

\chapter{Background}
\label{chap:background}

{\bf A compulsory chapter, roughly 20\% of the total page-count}
\vspace{1cm} 

\noindent
This chapter is intended to describe the technical basis on which execution
of the project depends.  The goal is to provide a detailed explanation of
the specific problem at hand, and existing work that is relevant such as an
existing algorithm that you use, alternative solutions proposed, supporting
technologies, and relevant literature. The literature you include should cover 
appropriate peer-reviewed academic publications and books, and maybe also 
news and current-affairs articles published in reputable sources such as 
{\em The Economist} magazine or {\em The Financial Times} newspaper. 

Your thesis should include complete set of references/bibliography: everything that you cite should be in there, in full. This means including the publisher's name for anything that is a book; the editors and title of books that a paper appears in as one item in a larger collection (e.g. proceedings volumes and/or thematic edited collections) and the relevant page-numbers; and so on. Put most simply, we require and expect your references to look like the references in a published peer-reviewed academic paper, so you can work out whether you still have work to do by comparing your references to the reference-list on the publications that you're working from. 

Note there is a subtle difference from
this and a full-blown literature {\em survey}.  The latter might try
to capture and organise (e.g., categorise somehow) {\em all}\/ related work,
potentially offering meta-analysis, whereas here the goal is simply to
ensure that your thesis is self-contained.  Put another way, after reading 
this chapter an intelligent non-expert reader with no prior knowledge of your project should have obtained enough background to 
understand what {\em you}\/ have done (by reading subsequent sections), and then 
accurately assess your work.  You might view an additional goal as giving 
the reader confidence that you are able to absorb, understand and clearly 
communicate highly technical material.

Just as there is no single ideal structure for an MSc thesis, there is no one correct name for this chapter. You could just call it {\em Background}\/ if you wish, or {\em Technical Background}. Or if you feel you have a lot to say you could split this chapter into two: you might have your first three chapters being:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Introduction
\item Context
\item Related Work
\end{enumerate}
\noindent
But if you prefer to use a more concise structure, you could instead use:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Introduction: Contextual Background
\item Technical Background
\end{enumerate}
\noindent
The choice is yours. 


The key thing is that by the end of these first two or three chapters you have told the reader everything they need to know so that they can understand the rest of your thesis. This is particularly important because at least one of the people who actually examines your thesis will be a UoB academic, one of our lecturers or professors, who you can assume knows almost nothing about the specifics of what work you have done, and who is given a copy of your thesis to mark. So what you write has to adequately explain things to that examiner. You can safely assume that whoever examines your thesis is numerate, intelligent, understands programming and data analytics etc, but you cannot safely assume that their specific individual expertise is a perfect match to the topic of your thesis (it's in that sense that the examiner is a {\em non-expert}). So, the person you write for, your {\em audience}, should be that undefined group of academics who might possibly examine your thesis: if you write for that audience and do a good job, your thesis should be understandable by a wide range of people, including potential employers and colleagues in the world of work.

\chapter{Execution}
\label{chap:execution}
This chapter describes the data and the methods used to detect, classify and denoise the target fibre's spikes in a single channel. It first introduces the recording and how the target unit was identified, then estimates the noise floor. It then describes the detection pipeline in three stages: building and matching a spike template, and constraining detections by their timing after the stimulus. It then describes the two classifiers used on the detection survivors: a one-class and two-class support vector machine. Finally describes three denoising methods: Wavelet, Dense Autoencoder and Convolutional Autoencoder to evaluate each method and to asses whether denoising improves detection or classification, and the metrics used throughout. 
\section{The data: rat saphenous nerve microneurography}
This project uses an extracellular microneurography recording from the peripheral nerve of a rat, sampled at 30 kHz over approximately 2.3 hours. The nerve carries many afferent fibres, so the electrode records the action potentials of many units at once, superimposed on background noise. Recordings were made using two multi-electrode sensor units placed at different positions along the nerve, each containing several
channels corresponding to slightly different electrode locations. Eight channels carry manually labelled ground truth spikes (CH3, CH8, CH9, CH10, CH14, CH19, CH25, CH30). During the recording, an electrical stimulus (Pain stimulus) was delivered repeatedly, giving 2,310 stimulation pulses (TTL) at a normal rate of around 0.25 Hz. The target fibre responds to each stimulus by firing at an approximately fixed latency. The ground truth spike times were marked manually by a human annotator \ref{fig:raw signal}, who only
labelled recognisable nerve responses; therefore, the annotations are conservative, and not every response is labelled. Each spike is marked at the point where the signal crosses zero on its descending slope.

\subsection{Target unit and channels}
The lab identified a single target unit as a strong unit with large identifiable spikes on multiple channels. This project analyses five of these channels: 9, 14, 19, 25, and 30, with a strong signal, and each is analysed independently. 
The spike latencies of the target unit were manually annotated on each channel; consequently, they are treated as reference annotations rather than complete ground truth, as they were manually identified and may contain errors. 
Only a 15-minute window was used for analyses, beginning at the 900th stimulus pulse. 
This window length was chosen because the target unit's latency remained stable; a longer window showed activity-dependent slowing that spreads the latency and prevents a fixed latency window from capturing the spikes. The signals were preprocessed by the recording laboratory, so it has been band-pass filtered 300-6000 Hz, common average referenced, and stimulus blanked. 
\section{Noise estimation and preprocessing}
As mentioned, no further filtering was applied in this project, because the recording given was already bandpass-filtered, with stimulation artefact blanked (set to zero). 
The target units are small, only about two to three times the amplitude of the background noise; consequently, they do not stand out clearly and can not be isolated by amplitude threshold alone. Therefore, removing the noise, detecting and classifying the target unit's spikes are the central challenges this chapter tries to address. The noise level is estimated using the median absolute deviation (MAD) \cite{quiroga_unsupervised_2004, park_deep_2019}:
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{\text{noise}} = \frac{\operatorname{median}(|x|)}{0.6745}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the signal. The median is used instead of standard deviation because the spikes are infrequent, so the median does not inflate the estimate the way standard deviation would; dividing by 0.6745 rescales the value to be comparable to a standard deviation under an assumption of Gaussian noise \cite{drebitz_optimizing_2019}. MAD is an estimate, so it assumes the signal is mostly noise and approximately Gaussian \cite{coupe_robust_2010}. But because it is applied identically to every signal, it is a valid basis for comparison.
\section{Leakage safe evaluation protocol}
For unbiased performance, a chronological split was used on the recording by dividing the recording into a development set on the first 70\% [3602.7-4232.7 s] and a held-out evaluation on the last 30\% of the recording [4232.7-4502.7 s]. All hyperparameter tuning was conducted on the development set, and the evaluation set was scored once to prevent data leakage and to avoid overfitting \cite{rashid_spike_2025}. All component that could carry information across the split was fitted on the development set only; the spike template was built from target spikes in the development annotations split only; the autoencoders were trained on synthetic data derived from the development set. Detection parameters like shape similarity and latency window were selected by maximising F1 on the development set. The classifiers and the two-class classifier were fitted on the development survivors only with fixed hyperparameters and scored once on the evaluation survivors. The confidence intervals were computed by stratified bootstrap resampling of the evaluation candidates.

The spike template was built by averaging the annotated unit's spikes on the development set, to prevent leakage in later stages. For each marked spike, a 90-sample window (45 samples on either side of the spike trough) is then extracted from the signal and then averaged to give the unit's mean waveform.

\section{Denoising quality}
The three denoisers were assessed across the five channels using two measures: MAD to assess each denoiser's noise reduction relative to the raw,  and the preservation of spike amplitude by using peak-to-peak amplitude relative to the raw signal. These measures were used instead of reconstructive error because no clean signal exists for these real recordings to compare against \cite{baldazzi_systematic_2020, adam_comparative_2025}
All denoisers reduced the noise level, as seen in Table \ref{tab:denoising quality}. Dense reduces the noise the most to MDA ~0.50, and the wavelet denoiser the least to ~2.30 compared to raw ~ 4.80. Despite the Dense autoencoder achieving the lowest MAD, it only retained 17-20\% of the raw spike amplitude across channels, making it the most destructive. The convolutional autoencoder reduced the noise and preserved the spike amplitude, achieving 55-57\% \ref{tab:denoising quality} \ref{fig:denosiing quality ch19}. \section{Template matching detection}
This section compares detection before and after denoising, comparing the three denoisers across the five channels. 
\subsection{Baseline detection}
 Detection was applied to the raw signal on each channel and scored against the reference annotations in the held-out evaluation period \ref{tab:Baseline detection}, \ref{fig:detection baseline}. The false positive to true positives ratio (FP/TP across all channels exceeded 1, ranging from 2.75 to 4.73, and recall was low, around 0.18 to 0.27. Detection on the raw signal of every channel showed more false positives than true positives. This indicates that these false positives resemble the target spikes in both waveforms and latency window, as they passed both the shape similarity threshold and the latency window. These results are consistent with the literature. According to \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026, hill_quality_2011} in single-electrode microneurography analysis, waveform shapes are highly similar across fibres, and it is difficult to separate overlapping units on a single electrode. The TP/FP ratio here for the raw signal is around 2.75 to 4.73, which is lower than those reported by \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026} for multi-fibre recordings; the FP/TP is around 2 to 20. However, these studies did not denoise the signal; the next subsection evaluates whether denoising the signal would make spike detection by shape and latency any better.

When comparing spike template matching detection before denoising to after denoising, none of the three denoisers reduced the FP/TP ratio to below 1 on any channel; even the largest improvement, which was dense on CH25, only improved it to 1.90 compared to the raw ratio of 3.14. The scattered pattern of improvement and worsening is quite balanced across the fifteen denoiser channel combinations. Several reasons and mechanisms reported in the literature for denoising increasing the number of false positives, and these fall into two categories. These false positives could be genuine biological spikes that were buried by the noise and were detected by the template matching after denoising, or artefacts introduced by the denoisers themselves.
According to \cite{bengio_generalized_2013}, denoising autoencoders can learn to pull corrupted points back to the manifold via a learned vector field, so whenever the model receives unstructured noise, it could reshape that noise into the nearest pattern, which is in this case spikes. This could be the reason for the false positives that were more compared to raw. Furthermore, denoising can diminish the spikes' fine features that help distinguish them from neighbouring units \cite{baldazzi_systematic_2020}, which in turn causes non-target spikes to resample the target template. If the additional detections (false positives) by the denoisers are actual spikes, they should look like the spike's waveforms in the raw signal; and if they are artefacts, they should be absent or near flat in the raw signal since they were created. This check was only done on ch19 for the dense autoencoder, where the FP/TP ratio increased from 4.73 to 7.00 with 11 additional detections. These additional detections have a mean trough of -11.77 in the raw signal, similar to the target spikes, indicating that these extra false positives might be spike-like events rather than artefacts produced by the denoiser. However, this can not rule out any other possibilities, as only the mean trough spikes were checked and only on one channel with one denoiser. Nonetheless, in a single channel, spikes do overlap, have similar waveform shapes and latency, and without the spatial information that of multi-channel analysis, it is difficult to distinguish target spikes from non-target \cite{troglio_hybrid_2026}.

This chapter is intended to evaluate what you did.  The content is highly 
topic-specific, but for many projects will have flavours of the following:

\begin{enumerate}
\item functional  testing, including analysis and explanation of failure 
      cases,
\item behavioural testing, often including analysis of any results that 
      draw some form of conclusion wrt. the aims and objectives,
      and
\item evaluation of options and decisions within the project, and/or a
      comparison with alternatives.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
This chapter often acts to differentiate project quality: even if the work
completed is of a high technical quality, critical yet objective evaluation 
and comparison of the outcomes is crucial.  In essence, the reader wants to
learn something, so the worst examples amount to simple statements of fact 
(e.g., ``graph X shows the result is Y''); the best examples are analytical 
and exploratory (e.g., ``graph X shows the result is Y, which means Z; this 
contradicts [1], which may be because I use a different assumption'').  As 
such, both positive {\em and}\/ negative outcomes are valid {\em if} presented 
in a suitable manner.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

{\bf A compulsory chapter,  roughly 10\% of the total page-count}
\vspace{1cm} 

\noindent
The concluding chapter(s) of a dissertation are often underutilized because they're 
too often left too close to the deadline: it is important to allocate enough time and 
attention to closing off the story, the narrative, of your thesis.

Again, there is no single correct way of closing a thesis. 

One good way of doing this is to have a single chapter consisting of three parts:

\begin{enumerate}
\item (Re)summarise the main contributions and achievements, in essence
      summing up the content.
\item Clearly state the current project status (e.g., ``X is working, Y 
      is not'') and evaluate what has been achieved with respect to the 
      initial aims and objectives (e.g., ``I completed aim X outlined 
      previously, the evidence for this is within Chapter Y'').  There 
      is no problem including aims which were not completed, but it is 
      important to evaluate and/or justify why this is the case.
\item Outline any open problems or future plans.  Rather than treat this
      only as an exercise in what you {\em could} have done given more 
      time, try to focus on any unexplored options or interesting outcomes
      (e.g., ``my experiment for X gave counter-intuitive results, this 
      could be because Y and would form an interesting area for further 
      study'' or ``users found feature Z of my software difficult to use,
      which is obvious in hindsight but not during at design stage; to 
      resolve this, I could clearly apply the technique of Bloggs {\em et al.}.
\end{enumerate}

Alternatively, you might want to divide this content into two chapters: a penultimate chapter with a title such as ``Further Work" and then a final chapter ``Conclusions". Again, there is no hard and fast rule, we trust you to make the right decision.
